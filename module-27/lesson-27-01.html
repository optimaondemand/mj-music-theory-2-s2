LESSON 27.01
Modulation: Moving Between Keys
Lesson
🎵 TODAY'S FOCUS
When music changes key, how do composers get from one tonal center to another? And how do analysts tell the difference between a brief visit and a full relocation?
Tonicization is a brief visit to another chord's neighborhood: one or two secondary dominants, then back home. Modulation is a full move: the music commits to a new key center, establishes it with a cadence, and stays long enough that the listener forgets where home was. This lesson covers three ways composers accomplish that move: the smooth pivot chord, the dream-like common-tone, and the abrupt direct modulation you hear in pop songs.

🎶 PART 1 · TONICIZATION VS. MODULATION

Both tonicization and modulation involve chords that point toward a pitch other than the main tonic. The difference is duration and commitment. A secondary dominant that resolves and moves on is tonicization. A passage that establishes a new key with a cadence and stays there long enough to feel settled is a modulation.

💡 THE RULE
If the new key is established with a cadence and multiple phrases, it is a modulation. If a secondary dominant resolves and the original key continues uninterrupted, it is tonicization only. When in doubt, ask: does the music feel like it has moved home, or just visited?
🎸 PART 2 · PIVOT CHORD MODULATION

The smoothest way to change keys is to find a chord that belongs to both the old key and the new key. That chord is the pivot chord. The listener hears it as part of the original key; the music then uses the new key's harmonic logic to cadence in the new key. The pivot is labeled with dual Roman numerals: its function in the old key on top, its function in the new key below.

How to find the pivot chord: (1) Identify where the new key begins (look for the first cadence in the new key or the first chord that only makes sense in the new key). (2) Look just before that point for the last chord that works in both the old and new key. (3) Label it with dual Roman numerals, old key on top and new key below. (4) Continue analysis in the new key from that chord forward.

⚡ COMMON-TONE MODULATION
A related technique (OMT p. 452): a single pitch is sustained while the harmony around it changes to the new key. The retained pitch provides a thread of continuity while the tonal center shifts. Common-tone modulations create a dream-like or floating effect. Listen for a held or repeated note while the chord underneath suddenly changes character.
🎶 PART 3 · DIRECT (CHROMATIC) MODULATION

A direct modulation (also called chromatic modulation) does not use a pivot chord. The key simply changes: one phrase ends in the old key, the next begins in the new key without preparation. The effect is abrupt, often energizing or dramatic. It is the most common modulation technique in pop and Romantic music.

⚠ HOW TO IDENTIFY IT
If you see a sudden key change (new key signature or a cluster of accidentals pointing to a new center) without a preceding pivot chord, you have a direct modulation. Look for: (1) a clear cadence in the old key; (2) an immediate start in the new key on the next downbeat; (3) no chord that can be labeled in both keys. Often the new key is just a half step or whole step higher than the old one.

💡 TAP EACH CARD to review the three modulation types:
Pivot chord modulation
(tap to review)
Smooth. A chord that functions in both the old and new key is reinterpreted as the listener's attention shifts to the new key. Label with dual Roman numerals: old key on top, new key below. Example: IV in C = I in F. Most common in Classical-era music.
Common-tone modulation
(tap to review)
Dream-like. A single pitch is held or repeated while the surrounding harmony changes to the new key. The retained note is the thread of continuity. The listener hears a floating shift as the old tonic dissolves under the held pitch.
Direct (chromatic) modulation
(tap to review)
Abrupt. No pivot. The old key cadences, and the new key simply begins. Creates a jolt of energy. Common in pop ("truck-driver" up a step for the final chorus) and Romantic music. Identify by: sudden new accidentals or key signature with no preparation in the preceding measure.

🤖 WORTH KNOWING · AI REHARMONIZATION AND ANALYTICAL JUDGMENT
An AI reharmonization tool can insert what looks like a modulation into a chord progression the moment you ask for one. Deciding whether a real passage in a piece of music is a true modulation or only a brief tonicization is an analytical judgment call, weighing duration, cadence, and whether the ear accepts a new home. That judgment is exactly what this lesson teaches, and no tool can settle it for you.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

Modulation = a sustained key change confirmed by a cadence. The three tools are: the pivot chord (smooth, dual Roman numeral label), the common-tone (dream-like, single held pitch), and the direct modulation (abrupt, no preparation, dramatic energy). When you analyze a score, ask first: has the key actually moved, or is this just a secondary dominant visiting?
