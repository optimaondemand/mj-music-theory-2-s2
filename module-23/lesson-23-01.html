LESSON 23.01
20th-Century Movements and Pitch Collections
Lesson
Today’s Focus
Where are we going?
Essential Question
How did composers in the 20th century organize music once they left behind the traditional V–I pull?

Tonal harmony ruled Western music for roughly three centuries: chords moved toward a home key, and the dominant-to-tonic cadence gave listeners a clear sense of direction and arrival. In the 20th century, composers began asking what music could sound like without that pull. Their answers produced several distinct movements, each with its own way of choosing and arranging pitches. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to identify those movements, name their key composers, and describe the pitch collections each one favored.

Part 1
A Map of 20th-Century Movements

No single technique replaced tonal harmony. Instead, several movements developed in overlapping waves across the century. The timeline below places each one roughly in time and identifies the composers and key characteristics that define it.

Notice that these movements overlapped. Serialism grew out of Expressionism; Neoclassicism was partly a reaction against Expressionist chaos; Minimalism arrived as a direct critique of the complexity that Serialism had produced. Knowing where each movement falls in time helps you understand why composers made the choices they did.

Key Vocabulary
Impressionism
Late 19th to early 20th century style emphasizing tone color, ambiguous harmony, whole-tone and pentatonic scales, and avoidance of strong cadences. Key composers: Debussy, Ravel.
Expressionism
Early 20th century style using extreme chromaticism, atonal harmony, fragmented forms, and intense emotional expression. Key composers: Schoenberg, Berg, Webern.
Neoclassicism
1920s to 1940s style returning to Classical-era forms and clarity while using modern harmonic language (modal, polytonal). Key composers: Stravinsky, Prokofiev.
Minimalism
Late 20th century style built on static harmony, gradual process, repetition, and steady pulse. A reaction against high-modernist complexity. Key composers: Reich, Glass, Adams.
Serialism
A technique using a predetermined ordering of all 12 pitches (the tone row) as the basis for all melodic and harmonic material. Developed by Schoenberg.
Pitch Collection
The set of pitches a composer draws from in a passage: pentatonic, whole-tone, octatonic, chromatic, or a tone row. Identifying the collection is the first step in post-tonal analysis.
Part 2
Impressionism

Impressionism in music shares its name with the painting movement (Monet, Renoir) for good reason: both arts prioritized atmosphere and color over clear structure and argument. Impressionist composers replaced the driving functional progressions of tonal harmony with something more floating and ambiguous.

Key Features

Impressionist composers used several techniques to achieve that floating quality:

  • Whole-tone and pentatonic scales instead of major/minor scales, removing the leading tone that creates the V–I pull.
  • Parallel chords (planing): moving a chord shape up or down without resolving it, treating chords as colors rather than functional steps.
  • Avoidance of strong cadences: phrases often dissolve rather than land with finality.
  • Rich orchestration and pedal effects: the piano sustain pedal blurs harmonies into a wash of sound.
Impressionism borrowed its name from painting. Just as Monet’s canvases captured light and atmosphere rather than sharp outlines, Debussy’s music captured mood and color rather than logical harmonic argument. The comparison was not entirely a compliment when first made, but both artists kept the name.
Composers and Public Domain Status

Claude Debussy (1862–1918) and Maurice Ravel (1875–1937) are the central Impressionist composers. Because both died more than 70 years ago, all of their works are in the public domain, and their scores are freely available.

One of the most famous Impressionist piano pieces. Notice the whole-tone inflections, the use of the sustain pedal to blend harmonies, and the absence of a strong dominant–tonic cadence at phrase ends.

Part 3
Expressionism and Serialism

Where Impressionism created a gentle blur, Expressionism pushed to the other extreme: maximum dissonance, maximum intensity, no harmonic home at all. The Second Viennese School (Schoenberg, Berg, and Webern) led this movement.

Expressionism

Expressionist music is characterized by:

  • Atonality: no single pitch functions as a home base or tonic.
  • Extreme dissonance: harsh intervals treated not as tensions to resolve but as the primary sound world.
  • Fragmented, compressed forms: some Webern pieces last under a minute.
  • Intense emotional expression: the music aimed to externalize inner psychological states directly.
Serialism: Organizing Atonality

Atonality was dramatic but hard to organize systematically. Schoenberg’s solution was the 12-tone technique (serialism): arrange all 12 pitches of the chromatic scale in a fixed order, called the tone row. Every pitch must appear exactly once before any pitch repeats. This gives all 12 pitches equal status, with no one pitch dominating as a tonic.

The composer can use the row forward (original), backward (retrograde), upside-down (inversion), or both at once (retrograde inversion). This gives a pool of 48 related versions of the row to draw from, all derived from the same source material. The logic is systematic, not random, but the result sounds nothing like tonal music.

Second Viennese School: Schoenberg, Berg, and Webern are grouped together as the Second Viennese School, distinguishing them from the First Viennese School of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven. All three worked in Vienna in the early 20th century and developed 12-tone and atonal techniques together.
Part 4
Neoclassicism and Minimalism
Neoclassicism

While the Second Viennese School was pushing into atonality, another group of composers reacted against Romantic excess in a different direction: back toward the clarity and formal logic of the Classical era, but with a modern harmonic vocabulary. This was Neoclassicism.

Neoclassical music uses:

  • Classical and Baroque forms: sonata form, concerto, fugue, passacaglia.
  • Cleaner textures: fewer instruments, leaner counterpoint, no post-Romantic orchestral sprawl.
  • Polytonal or modal harmony: tonal-sounding but with clashing key centers or modal scales instead of traditional major/minor.

Igor Stravinsky’s Pulcinella Suite (1920) is a landmark example. Stravinsky took music attributed to the Baroque composer Pergolesi and rewrote it with his own sharper, more dissonant harmonic language, keeping the form and energy of the original while making it unmistakably modern.

Minimalism

By the 1960s, composers like Steve Reich, Philip Glass, and John Adams felt that music had become so complex (dense serial scores, extended techniques, elaborate notation) that it had lost its audience entirely. Minimalism stripped music down to almost nothing.

Minimalist characteristics:

  • Very few pitches and chords: a piece might use only a handful of notes for its entire duration.
  • Gradual process: change happens slowly, often so slowly that listeners catch it only in retrospect.
  • Steady pulse: a clear, regular beat (a deliberate contrast to the irregular, complex rhythms of serialism).
  • Phasing (Steve Reich): two identical patterns start in unison, then one gradually moves ahead of the other, creating shifting layers.
Minimalism’s reach: Minimalism has deeply influenced film scores (think of Philip Glass’s work for Koyaanisqatsi), electronic music, ambient music, and much of what is now called “indie classical.” Composers like Max Richter and Johann Johannsson work directly in its legacy.
Part 5 · OMT p. 689
Analyzing with Collections

OMT page 689 gives a framework for analyzing post-tonal music. Because you cannot assume a tonal center, you start somewhere different than Roman numeral analysis.

  1. 1
    Identify the pitch collection. What pitches does the passage actually use? Count them. Do they form a pentatonic scale (5 notes, no semitones)? A whole-tone scale (6 notes, all whole steps)? An octatonic scale (8 notes, alternating half and whole steps)? The full chromatic (all 12)? A tone row?
  2. 2
    Look for patterns and repetition. Does a melodic fragment repeat exactly, transposed, inverted, or in different voices? Repetition is the structural glue when harmonic function is absent.
  3. 3
    Describe the organization without assuming a tonal center. Instead of "the piece is in G major," say "the piece uses a pentatonic collection centered around G" or "the passage is based on a whole-tone collection."

Summary
The One Thing to Remember
Key Takeaway
20th-century music organized itself through pitch collections (Impressionism used whole-tone and pentatonic; Expressionism went fully chromatic), serialism (12-tone rows gave every pitch equal status without a tonic), and gradual process (Minimalism built complexity from simple, repeating patterns). Traditional V–I progressions were not abolished everywhere at once: they were replaced in different ways by different movements at different times. Knowing which collection a piece draws from is the starting point for any post-tonal analysis.
