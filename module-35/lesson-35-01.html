🎶 PART 1 · THE AABA 32-BAR FORM

The most common jazz standard form is AABA: four sections of eight bars each, totaling 32 bars. The A sections establish the main theme. The B section (the bridge) provides harmonic contrast, often in a new key. The final A brings the theme back, sometimes with a different ending to stop the tune or loop it.

AABA Form Map, 32 Bars Total

The head is your composed melody. In a jazz performance, the band plays the head at the start, then each soloist improvises through as many 32-bar choruses as they like, then the band plays the head again to close. Your job as a composer is to write a head that is memorable enough to frame the improvisation.

💡 KEY POINT
The A sections use first and second endings. The first ending leads into the repeat (back to A) or into the bridge. The second ending closes the tune or returns to bar 1 for another chorus. Mark these in your lead sheet.
🎸 PART 2 · ii-V-I BACKBONE AND THE TURNAROUND

The ii-V-I progression is the DNA of jazz harmony. A minor-7th chord on the supertonic (ii) moves to a dominant-7th chord on the dominant (V), then resolves to the major-7th chord on the tonic (I). This three-chord motion appears multiple times in most A sections and is the standard approach to a key center.

The ii-V-I Progression (in C major)

At the end of each A section, bars 7-8 carry the turnaround: a two-bar progression that propels the ear back to bar 1. The most common turnaround is I - vi - ii - V (in C: Cmaj7 - Am7 - Dm7 - G7). The vi and ii fill the space between I and the returning V, adding forward momentum.

The Turnaround: I-vi-ii-V (bars 7-8 of each A section, in C major)

The turnaround makes bar 8 land on V7 (G7 in C major), which creates tension that resolves beautifully back to I at bar 1. On the final A section, you can replace the turnaround with a tag ending (repeating the last two bars) or a simple I chord to close.

💡 BRIDGE DESIGN TIP
A common bridge approach: move to the key of IV (the subdominant) and use a ii-V-I in that key for the first 4 bars, then use a ii-V that leads back to the original key for the last 4 bars. Example: in C major, the bridge might use ii-V-I in F major (Gm7 - C7 - Fmaj7) for bars 1-4, then Am7 - D7 to lead back to G7-Cmaj7.
🎹 PART 3 · WRITING THE HEAD MELODY

A jazz head melody must be singable, memorable, and flexible enough to invite improvisation. Here are the principles that guide professional jazz melody writing, grounded in the OMT discussion of jazz melody and voicing (p. 538-555).

⚡ TAP EACH CARD to explore a jazz melody principle:
Chord tones on strong beats
(tap to reveal)
rule
Place chord tones (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th of the current chord) on beat 1 and beat 3. These beats carry the harmonic weight. Non-chord tones work as passing tones or approach tones on beats 2 and 4 and on off-beats.
Step-wise approach tones
(tap to reveal)
rule
Land on chord tones by step from a half or whole step above or below. These approach tones (a step away) create smooth voice leading in the melody. A melody that leaps constantly without step-wise motion feels hard to sing and hard to follow.
Singable range
(tap to reveal)
rule
Keep the melody within a tenth (an octave plus a third) if possible. Leaps larger than an octave are rare and should serve an expressive purpose. A head that sits in a comfortable range for a vocalist or soloist is more likely to become a standard.
Motivic unity
(tap to reveal)
rule
Use a short rhythmic or melodic cell (a motive) and develop it across the A sections. Vary it in the bridge. Motivic unity makes your head feel composed rather than improvised, and gives soloists material to reference during their solos.
🎶 BRIDGE MELODY CONTRAST
Because the bridge moves to a new key, the melody should feel different: a higher register, a new rhythmic feel, or a new melodic shape. This contrast is what makes the return of the A section so satisfying. Think of it as: A sections = home, bridge = departure, final A = return.

✍️ YOUR COMPOSITION TASK

Your task is to write an original 32-bar jazz head in AABA form. You are composing a new jazz standard, not transcribing an existing one. Your lead sheet will show: the melody (in treble clef) and the chord symbols above the staff. You do not need to write inner voices or bass lines.

COMPOSITION REQUIREMENTS
1
AABA form: 32 bars, four 8-bar sections. Label each section (A1, A2, B, A3) on your lead sheet.
2
At least one ii-V-I in each A section, written as chord symbols. Include a turnaround (bars 7-8 of each A section).
3
A bridge that moves to a contrasting key (you choose which key) and returns to the original key by bar 7 of the bridge.
4
A singable, memorable head melody with chord tones on strong beats. The melody can be simple. It does not need to be virtuosic.
5
Composer statement (4-6 sentences): name the key, describe your bridge modulation, list any extensions or substitutions used, and describe the melodic character you aimed for. What does your jazz head express?

Format: Submit your lead sheet as a MuseScore file, a PDF, or a clear photograph of handwritten staff paper. Your composer statement goes in the Canvas text box. Both are required.

If you would like to include a recording of the head, you are welcome to, but it is not required. The lead sheet and statement are the deliverable.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

Jazz composition in AABA form rests on three pillars: the 32-bar AABA structure (A establishes the key, B contrasts and departs, final A returns), the ii-V-I progression as the harmonic engine, and a singable head melody with chord tones on strong beats. Your original jazz head is where those principles become something that musicians can play and make their own.
