Extended Counterpoint and Fugue
Lesson
🎵 TODAY'S FOCUS
When the same melody appears in different voices, different keys, even upside down and slowed way down, how does a fugue keep it all sounding like one unified piece?
A fugue takes a single melody called the subject and puts it through one of the most rigorous forms in all of music. Every voice enters with that subject in turn during the exposition. Then episodes carry fragments of it between key areas. Stretto crowds the entries on top of each other near the end. Throughout, augmentation and inversion transform the subject while keeping its identity intact. This lesson maps the whole structure so you can follow a fugue from first note to last.

🎶 PART 1 · THE FUGAL EXPOSITION

A fugue opens with an exposition. Each voice enters one at a time with the same melody. The first voice states the subject in the home key (tonic). When the second voice enters with the subject, it does so a fifth higher, in the dominant key. That transposed version is called the answer. While the second voice sings the answer, the first voice continues with a new accompanying melody called the countersubject. Additional voices follow, alternating between subject (tonic) and answer (dominant), until all voices have entered.

The canonical example of every fugue technique in this lesson is Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (public domain, free on IMSLP). Book I alone contains 24 fugues covering every major and minor key. Follow a score while you listen and you will hear everything in this lesson in action.

💡 TONAL VS. REAL ANSWER
A real answer is an exact transposition of the subject up a perfect fifth. A tonal answer adjusts one or more intervals so the music stays grounded in the home key rather than implying a new key center. Most fugue subjects that begin on scale degree 5 use a tonal answer. The adjustment is subtle, but it keeps the tonal architecture stable during the exposition.
🎸 PART 2 · EPISODE, STRETTO, AND SUBJECT TRANSFORMATIONS

After the exposition, fugues typically alternate between episodes and middle entries. An episode does not state the full subject. Instead, it takes fragments of the subject and sequences them, passing them between voices and moving through different key areas. Episodes release tension between subject entries and create forward momentum.

Stretto (OMT p. 259) appears near the end of many fugues. Voices enter with the subject so quickly that each new entry begins before the previous one has finished. The effect is crowding, excitement, and a sense that the music is rushing toward a conclusion. Stretto is one of the most recognizable signs that a fugue is approaching its close.

⚡ SUBJECT TRANSFORMATIONS
Augmentation: All note values are doubled. A quarter note becomes a half note, a half note becomes a whole note. The subject slows down and sounds grander, more monumental.
Inversion: Every interval is reversed in direction. A rising third becomes a falling third; a falling fourth becomes a rising fourth. The subject moves in the opposite direction at every step, yet its rhythmic identity makes it recognizable. These transformations add variety while keeping motivic unity intact throughout a long work.

🤖 WORTH KNOWING · AI AND FUGUE ANALYSIS
Some AI music tools can generate a fugue-like texture in moments, complete with entries that imitate each other. Producing that sound is not the same as being able to trace a real fugue's subject, answer, and episodes by ear and on the page, which is the skill this lesson builds. A generated imitation will not teach a student to hear that structure the way working through a real score, like Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier, does.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

A fugue opens with an exposition (subject in tonic, answer in dominant, countersubject against the answer), continues with episodes that fragment the subject in sequence, intensifies through stretto (overlapping entries), and often transforms the subject through augmentation or inversion. The subject's identity persists through all transformations. That is the miracle of fugal writing.
