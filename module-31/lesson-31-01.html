🎵 TODAY'S FOCUS
When you hear a piece of music, how do you figure out which mode it is in?
You have studied all seven diatonic modes. Now it is time to put that knowledge to work: listening for the tonal center, spotting the characteristic note that gives each mode away, and recognizing the looping chord patterns that show up in folk and rock music. By the end of this lesson you will have a reliable method for mode identification and a comparison of modal analysis with the Roman numeral analysis you already know.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the mode of a given passage by finding the tonal center, building the scale, locating the characteristic note or chord, and naming the mode.
2
I will be able to analyze a modal folk or rock piece for its mode and identify its characteristic harmonic loop.
3
I will be able to explain how modal analysis differs from functional Roman numeral analysis.

🎶 PART 1 · THE FOUR-STEP IDENTIFICATION METHOD
Mode Identification: Four-Step Flowchart
Follow the four steps top to bottom, then trace down to the correct mode box.

The flowchart above gives you the full procedure. Here is how to use each step in practice.

Step 1 - Find the tonal center. Listen for the pitch that feels like home. It is the note the music returns to, rests on, and often begins or ends with. In a notated excerpt, look for the final note of phrases, repeated bass notes, and pedal tones.

Step 2 - Build the scale on that pitch. Collect all the pitches used in the passage and arrange them in stepwise order starting from your tonal center. Write them out: do they match any of the seven mode patterns?

Step 3 - Find the characteristic note or chord. This is the identification shortcut. Each mode has one scale degree or one chord that gives it away immediately. Once you know which note or chord is distinctive, you can name the mode quickly.

Step 4 - Name the mode. Match what you found in Step 3 to the chart. Confirm by checking the full scale degree set.

💡 KEY POINT
The tonal center does not have to be C. A piece can be in D Dorian, G Mixolydian, or B♭ Phrygian. What matters is finding the pitch that functions as home, then comparing the surrounding pitches to the mode patterns.
🎸 PART 2 · CHARACTERISTIC NOTE AND CHORD SUMMARY
Mode Characteristic Notes and Chord Clues

Notice the pattern of comparisons in the table. Dorian and Phrygian and Aeolian all share the same basic minor character (lowered 3rd and 7th compared to major), but each has a distinguishing alteration. Dorian raises the 6th one half step above Aeolian. Phrygian lowers the 2nd one half step below Aeolian. That single degree is all it takes to change the mode entirely.

Similarly, Lydian and Mixolydian both relate to major. Lydian raises the 4th one half step above Ionian. Mixolydian lowers the 7th one half step below Ionian. Locrian is the outlier: it has both ♭2 and ♭5, giving it a diminished fifth above the tonic. That tritone instability is why Locrian almost never appears as a full tonal center in real music.

⚡ TAP EACH CARD for the characteristic-note shortcut for each mode:
Dorian
(tap to reveal shortcut)
♯6 Raised 6th above the tonal center. Compare to natural minor: if scale degree 6 is raised by a half step, it is Dorian. The IV chord will be major (not minor as in Aeolian).
Phrygian
(tap to reveal shortcut)
♭2 Lowered 2nd above the tonal center. The pitch right above home is only a half step away. Listen for the characteristic slide down from ♭2 to 1. The ♭II chord is the signature harmony.
Lydian
(tap to reveal shortcut)
♯4 Raised 4th (tritone above tonic). Compare to major: if scale degree 4 is raised by a half step, it is Lydian. The II major chord appears where major would have ii minor.
Mixolydian
(tap to reveal shortcut)
♭7 Lowered 7th (subtonic, whole step below tonic). Compare to major: if scale degree 7 is lowered by a half step, it is Mixolydian. The ♭VII chord is the signature harmony.
Locrian
(tap to reveal shortcut)
♭2 + ♭5 Both the 2nd AND the 5th are lowered. The fifth above the tonic is diminished. This is the only mode where the tonic triad is a diminished chord (i°).
Dorian vs. Aeolian
(tap to reveal the difference)
one note The only difference between Dorian and Aeolian (natural minor) is scale degree 6. If you hear a minor-sounding piece with a major IV chord, it is Dorian. If IV is minor, it is Aeolian.
🎶 PART 3 · MODAL SCHEMAS AND HARMONIC LOOPS

Modal music often relies on schemas: short, repeating chord patterns that establish and reinforce the mode. These loops are different from functional tonal harmony. In tonal music, chords serve roles (T-PD-D) that create directed motion toward a cadence. In modal music, the loop itself is the point. Chords circle without necessarily resolving to a strong dominant-tonic cadence.

Three Common Modal Schemas

These three schemas appear constantly in folk and rock music. You will hear the Mixolydian loop in many rock songs that use a major scale but avoid the leading-tone pull. The Dorian loop appears in jazz-influenced folk and Celtic music. The Aeolian progression (sometimes called the Andalusian cadence when it descends further) is one of the most common patterns in popular music.

💡 KEY POINT
A modal loop does not have a single correct stopping point. The loop can repeat 2 times or 20 times. The characteristic chord (the ♭VII in Mixolydian, the major IV in Dorian) is what tells you which mode you are in, not the length or the ending.
🎶 PART 4 · MODAL VS. TONAL ANALYSIS

You have been doing Roman numeral analysis all year: labeling chords by scale degree and describing their function (Tonic, Pre-Dominant, Dominant). That system works well for tonal music: music where the harmonies have directed motion toward a goal cadence.

Modal music uses the same Roman numerals, but the functions are different. Look at the comparison below.

C Major (Ionian) vs. C Mixolydian: Same Roman Numerals, Different Meaning

Both C major and C Mixolydian use I, IV, and the same tonal center. But C major also has V (with leading tone B natural), while C Mixolydian has ♭VII (with subtonic B♭). The difference in that one note changes the entire harmonic function of the chord and the feeling of the music.

In tonal analysis, you label the function of a chord: Tonic, Pre-Dominant, Dominant. In modal analysis, you describe the color and pattern: which loop is present, which characteristic chord confirms the mode, and how many times the loop repeats. Both uses of Roman numerals are correct; they just answer different questions about the music.

💡 KEY POINT
When you see a Roman numeral like IV in a modal piece, it does not automatically mean Pre-Dominant function. Ask: does this chord lead somewhere with directed tension, or does it color the loop and return to the tonic? The answer tells you whether to use functional labels or modal pattern labels.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

Mode identification uses four steps: find the tonal center, build the scale, find the characteristic note or chord, name the mode. The characteristic notes are the shortcuts: Dorian = ♯6, Phrygian = ♭2, Lydian = ♯4, Mixolydian = ♭7, Locrian = ♭2 plus ♭5. Modal analysis describes color and loop patterns; tonal analysis describes functional roles. Both use Roman numerals, but they answer different questions.
