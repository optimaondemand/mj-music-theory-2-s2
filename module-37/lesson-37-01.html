LESSON 37.01
Theory II Year-End Portfolio
Lesson
🎵 TODAY'S FOCUS
Theory teaches us the grammar of music. But like all grammar, it serves expression. What will you do with the musical grammar you have learned?
The Year-End Portfolio is not a test. It is a chance to look back at the full arc of Music Theory II, choose the work you are most proud of, reflect on what it shows about your growth, and point toward what comes next. You are the curator; you decide what matters and why.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to select 3-5 pieces of work that best represent my growth across the full course, with at least one from Q1-Q2 and one from Q3-Q4.
2
I will be able to write a specific reflection for each piece: what it demonstrates, what was hard, and what I would do differently.
3
I will be able to identify a specific music learning goal and write a 3-step action plan for pursuing it after this course.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your completed assignments, compositions, ear-training screenshots, and analyses from all four quarters. Pull them together before you start this lesson.
▶
A new Word document (Microsoft 365) for your portfolio write-ups and reflections.
▶
Open Music Theory v2 (viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory) bookmarked for continued reference after the course ends.
🎶 PART 1 · CURATING YOUR PORTFOLIO

A portfolio is not "everything you did." It is a curated selection: 3-5 pieces chosen to tell the story of your growth. The best portfolios show range across types of work: analytical, compositional, and ear-training. The diagram below shows how to think about your selection.

Solid border = required slot. Dashed border = optional. Choose for range, not just for ease.
💡 WHAT MAKES A STRONG PORTFOLIO CHOICE
Choose work that you can say something real about, not just work that got a good grade. A piece where you struggled and then figured something out is often a better portfolio item than a piece that came easily. The reflection is what makes the portfolio; without it, it is just a file folder.

Aim for one analytical item (a harmonic analysis, a form analysis, a counterpoint exercise, a listening analysis), one compositional item (a melody, a harmonization, your multi-section composition), and one ear-training achievement (a musictheory.net or teoria.com screenshot showing your best result, or a sight-singing recording). The fourth and fifth items can be anything that shows a different dimension of your growth.

🎸 PART 2 · WRITING A GENUINE REFLECTION

A portfolio reflection is not a summary of what you did. It is an explanation of what the work means and what you learned from doing it. Here is the difference:

WEAK REFLECTION (summary)
"In the first semester I learned about jazz harmony. I did the ii-V-I assignment. I got a good grade."
This describes the task. It does not say anything about thinking or growth.
STRONG REFLECTION (meaning)
"I chose my ii-V-I analysis because it was the first time I heard a chord-scale relationship as something that explains why a solo sounds right, not just as a rule. What was hard was understanding why the Mixolydian scale fits V7 specifically. What I would do differently is try to sing each scale while playing the chord, since the ear connection was missing from my first attempt."
This identifies a specific insight, names the difficulty, and points toward future growth.

For each piece in your portfolio, answer the three questions in complete sentences. Use the name of the specific skill or concept (do not just say "music theory"). Write as if you are explaining your thinking to someone who has never taken this course but is genuinely curious about what you learned.

📄 THE THREE REFLECTION QUESTIONS
1. What specific skill or concept does this piece of work demonstrate?
2. What was hard about it at the time?
3. What would you do differently now, knowing what you know?
🎹 PART 3 · IN-DEPTH PRESENTATION (POSTED RECORDING)

Choose one item from your portfolio for an in-depth walkthrough. This is your chance to explain your analytical or compositional thinking out loud. You will post a recording and reply to one classmate's recording.

1
Choose one item from your portfolio for the in-depth walkthrough. Pick the one you have the most to say about.
2
Record your walkthrough (3-5 minutes): talk through the specific choices in the work. For an analysis: name each chord and explain why it functions the way it does. For a composition: explain why you chose a particular chord, mode, or formal section. Focus on 2-3 specific moments in the work rather than trying to cover everything.
3
Post your recording in the Portfolio Share Discussion thread and reply to one classmate: name one specific thing you found insightful or heard differently, and ask a genuine question about their work or thinking.
🎯 PART 4 · YOUR MUSIC LEARNING GOAL

The portfolio ends with a look forward. Open Music Theory v2 (all parts, free at viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory) will still be there after this course ends. Parts V, IX, and X cover topics beyond Theory II: extended chromaticism, post-tonal theory, and more. Name one concrete next step and write a 3-step plan for getting there.

A specific goal + a first action + a free resource = a plan you can actually follow.
📚 FREE RESOURCES AFTER THIS COURSE
Open Music Theory v2 at viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory (Parts V, IX, and X go beyond Theory II). teoria.com and musictheory.net for continued ear training. IMSLP for free public-domain scores. YouTube for guided analysis walkthroughs of any piece you want to study. These are all free and will still be there in five years.

🤖 WORTH KNOWING · AI AND MUSIC COPYRIGHT
AI-generated music and audio raise real, current questions about copyright and attribution, which is part of why this year's course included a reading on music law and the safe, legal use of media. AI tools could also draft a generic portfolio reflection for you. But the portfolio is specifically about your own growth and judgment across the year, and a reflection that did not come from your own thinking would not show that.

You have spent a full year moving through one of the most demanding subjects in music education.
