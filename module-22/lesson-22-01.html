Whole-Tone and Octatonic Collections
Lesson
🎵 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens to music when harmony stops relying on the pull of the dominant toward the tonic?
Tonal music is organized around keys, scales, and chords that move toward or away from a home pitch. Two collections, the whole-tone scale and the octatonic (diminished) scale, are built differently. They divide the octave into equal slices, which means there is no unique leading tone and no single note that serves as the inevitable home. Today you build both collections, study their symmetrical structure, and look at how Debussy and Stravinsky used them.

🎶 PART 1 · THE WHOLE-TONE SCALE

A whole-tone scale is built entirely from whole steps: every note to the next is exactly a major second (M2). There are no half steps anywhere. The result is a scale of six pitches that divides the octave into six equal slices. Because all the intervals are the same size, the scale sounds floating and directionless. Claude Debussy used it to create harmonies that shimmer without moving anywhere in particular (OMT p. 684).

All six intervals are whole steps. The scale generates only augmented triads because stacking two major thirds (which are also two whole steps each) always produces an augmented fifth. No perfect fourths or fifths appear anywhere in the scale.

Notice what is missing: no half steps means no leading tone, no dominant-tonic pull, and no perfect fourth or fifth. Because the scale is made entirely of equal M2 intervals, every pitch in it could feel like the tonic. The harmony floats.

Symmetry: The whole-tone scale divides the octave into 6 equal major second slices. Each slice is the same size, so the pattern repeats at every M2 interval. Transpose the scale up a whole step and you get the same six pitches in a different order.
💡 KEY POINT
Only two whole-tone scales exist. Every pitch in the chromatic scale belongs to either the C whole-tone collection (C–D–E–F♯–G♯–B♭) or the C♯ whole-tone collection (C♯–D♯–F–G–A–B). Any starting pitch you choose will land you in one of these two groups.
🎶 PART 2 · THE OCTATONIC SCALE

The octatonic scale has eight notes built by alternating half steps and whole steps: H–W–H–W–H–W–H–W. (Some sources start with W–H instead; both are valid rotations of the same pattern.) It is also called the diminished scale in jazz because it fits over diminished seventh chords and altered dominant chords. Igor Stravinsky, Bartók, and many jazz improvisers have made heavy use of it (OMT p. 686).

Red HS = half step; green W = whole step. The pattern H–W alternates all the way through all 8 notes. The scale shown is oct(0,1) starting on C: C–D♭–E♭–E–F♯–G–A–B♭–C.

The octatonic scale contains four diminished seventh chords and two dominant seventh chords, which is why it fits so naturally over those harmonies. In jazz the H–W version (starting with half step) is used over a diminished seventh chord, while the W–H version is used over an altered dominant chord.

Symmetry: The octatonic scale repeats at every minor third (m3). Transpose the collection up three half steps and you get the same eight pitches in a different rotation. This means there are only three distinct octatonic collections, no matter what starting pitch you use.
💡 KEY POINT
The octatonic scale is the same scale jazz musicians call the diminished scale. You will encounter it labeled both ways depending on whether you are studying classical post-tonal theory or jazz theory. Same pattern, different names.
⚙️ PART 3 · SYMMETRY COMPARISON

Both scales are symmetrical: they divide the octave into equal repeated units. The comparison below shows exactly how each one slices the octave and why that eliminates a single tonal center.

The whole-tone circle has 6 equal sectors (each a M2); the octatonic circle has 4 sectors (each a m3 grouping of 2–3 notes). In both cases the equal division means no single sector stands out as the tonal center.

In a major or minor scale, scale degree 7 (the leading tone) is a half step below the tonic. That half-step tension creates a gravitational pull toward the tonic. In a symmetrical scale, because every starting point is an equal distance from every other, that pull disappears. You can begin a phrase on any note and it will feel equally like home, or equally like not-home. The music hovers.

🎜 PART 4 · IMPRESSIONISM AND SCORE ANALYSIS

Impressionism in music (roughly 1890–1920) borrowed the term from French painting. Composers in this style, especially Claude Debussy (1862–1918) and Maurice Ravel (1875–1937), were more interested in color and atmosphere than in harmonic drive. Rather than building a sense of motion toward a goal, they layered chords and scales for their sound quality alone.

The whole-tone scale suited this perfectly. Because it contains no perfect fourth, perfect fifth, or half step, it generates no functional dominant. Harmony built from the whole-tone scale cannot move in the standard V–I pattern. It simply exists, shimmering. Debussy described it as music freed from the tyranny of the bar line and the cadence.

Ravel's works (all public domain as of 2008) use both the whole-tone and the pentatonic collection in similar ways. You may explore them on IMSLP as well.

Jazz and the octatonic: Jazz musicians named the octatonic the diminished scale because it fits directly over a diminished seventh chord. The chord tones C–E♭–F♯–A are all inside the C octatonic collection. Improvisers also use the W–H rotation of the scale (starting with a whole step) over an altered dominant seventh chord (7♯9, 7♭9, etc.). You will hear this sound in bebop and post-bop recordings throughout the mid-20th century.

💪 THE ONE THING TO REMEMBER

The whole-tone scale (6 notes, all whole steps, only 2 transpositions) and the octatonic scale (8 notes, H–W alternating, 3 transpositions) are both symmetrical: they divide the octave into equal repeated units. That symmetry means no single pitch functions as an inevitable tonic and no dominant-to-tonic pull exists. The whole-tone scale generates augmented triads and a floating, coloristic sound; the octatonic scale generates diminished seventh chords and a dense, angular sound. Debussy favored the whole-tone; Stravinsky, Bartók, and jazz musicians favored the octatonic.
